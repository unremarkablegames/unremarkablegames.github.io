# Unremarkable Games: voice reference

This is an internal editorial reference for maintaining the website. It records
Karl's direction from the site's refresh. It is not website copy or a manifesto
to publish. Keep this file excluded from Jekyll's output.

## The person and the studio

- Karl McClendon runs Unremarkable Games in Minnesota. He is an experienced
  programmer who left big tech to make games.
- Roslyn is Karl's daughter. She helps with events, development, and Neon
  Labyrinth while finishing college at Moody Bible Institute. Do not describe
  the studio as an equal partnership or imply that she jointly runs it.
- Use Karl's first-person voice for the studio's direction, beliefs, creative
  work, and correspondence. Use “we” only where it accurately describes shared
  work or the business as an entity. Do not mechanically rewrite legal policies.
- A small studio does not mean inexperienced, amateur, or low quality. Avoid
  underdog pitches, beginner language, and apologies for the studio's size.
- Personal information shared during discussion is not automatically material
  for a public biography. Include only what belongs in the requested copy.

## Tone

Write as a person who makes things and is introducing the work. The website
helps people find and understand the games; it does not need MBA positioning,
customer archetypes, conversion rhetoric, or a manufactured brand persona.

Keep the writing casual, direct, specific, and confident. Contractions are fine.
Prefer an actual detail about a game or its development to claims about meaning,
craftsmanship, authenticity, or emotional impact. Let the reader judge the work.

The name has dry, understated humor. Karl's own description is “I am
unremarkable.” The line “I'm the unremarkable part” fits that sensibility.
Keep the joke occasional and unforced. It is not a claim that the games are
poorly made, and it does not require a punchline about secretly being remarkable.

Avoid corporate enthusiasm, grand mission statements, affected profundity,
angsty confessions, campiness, and constant self-deprecation. Do not turn
uncertainty about an unfinished story into insecurity about professional ability.

## Christianity and the work

Karl is a born-again Christian. His faith is integrated into who he is and how
he sees the world. It is not a detachable influence, an interest alongside other
interests, a club membership, or something added to a game afterward.

State his Christianity plainly where introducing him. A useful formulation is:
“I'm a Christian. That's inseparable from how I see the world and what I make.”
Do not repeat that explanation across every page.

- Do not position the studio as filling a market gap for “Jesus games.”
- Do not imply that the usual work is devotional, biblical instruction, or a
  retelling of Bible stories. Explicitly spiritual projects remain possible;
  describe each project on its own terms.
- “Christianity in solution” describes integration, not a strategy for hiding
  Christian signals. Do not add verses, symbols, church scenery, or religious
  explanations to certify the work. Religious material may belong when the
  particular work calls for it.
- Karl dislikes allegory and prefers not to assign characters, events, or worlds
  theological counterparts. Do not invite readers to decode the games or
  invent spiritual explanations for their mechanics or artwork.
- Do not equate Christian authorship with squeaky-clean content, suitability
  for all ages, comforting outcomes, or a promise never to offend. There is room
  for real good, real evil, ambiguity, and flawed people. Content guidance must
  describe the individual game accurately.

Tolkien, Andrew Peterson, and Madeleine L'Engle are useful creative references.
Karl's comparison is closer to *The Lord of the Rings* or *Wingfeather* than to
an explicitly devotional work such as *Behold the Lamb of God*. These are
references for understanding his approach, not comparisons of stature to put
in promotional copy.

## Discovery, audience, and meaning

Karl wants to be faithful to the work and discover what it becomes. Do not force
him to define a theme, theological meaning, or intended player emotion before
the work has taken shape. Do not invent those things on his behalf.

His account of CyberTank2K is concrete: he started with a setting, added the
manager, and now has characters and a story to uncover. Keep that account on
the game page rather than repeating it throughout the site.

The games welcome anyone who wants a good game. People seeking work from a
Christian imagination may particularly connect with them. “Little lights in
the darkness” expresses Karl's hope; it is not a guaranteed emotional benefit
or a requirement to turn the site's lit window into a religious metaphor.

Some games are simply enjoyable arcade games. They need no theological
justification and should not be treated as lesser work. Let each game establish
its own subject, scope, and tone.

## Applying the voice

- Homepage: show the games, then introduce Karl briefly and plainly.
- Studio page: describe who runs the studio and his approach to making things.
- Game pages: explain the actual play, status, platforms, and relevant content.
  Include specific development context where it helps.
- Notes: allow room for process, discoveries, and uncertainty in Karl's voice.
  Preserve historical context in dated writing.
- Keep released games, work in development, and past prototypes distinct.
  Voidstone was a prototype; do not present it as an upcoming release.
- Keep the retro pixel-art and Tokyo midnight visual character. Do not attach
  symbolic interpretations to the design merely to express faith.
- Preserve Apple-facing support and privacy URLs. Keep factual and legal claims
  grounded in verified behavior; stylistic consistency is not a reason to invent
  or strengthen a promise.

When reviewing copy, check for repetition, unsupported promises, forced jokes,
and claims that outrun the work. More research or introspection is not always
needed: make a restrained draft from what is known, then let Karl react to it.
